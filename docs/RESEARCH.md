# The research behind Hero Academy

Hero Academy was built for one specific learner: an 8-year-old in third grade who already knew his 1s, 5s, 9s, 10s and 11s, but still counted up to answer anything involving 3, 4, 6, 7 or 8. Every major design decision in the game traces back to the research summarized here.

This document covers what the evidence says, how strong it is, where researchers disagree, and exactly how each finding shows up in the game.

---

## Starting point: the problem is smaller than it looks

Multiplication is commutative (6 × 7 = 7 × 6). Once a child knows their 1s, 5s, 9s, 10s and 11s, the only facts left in the standard table are the 3s, 4s, 6s, 7s and 8s multiplied by each other: **15 facts** (3×3, 3×4, 3×6, 3×7, 3×8, 4×4, 4×6, 4×7, 4×8, 6×6, 6×7, 6×8, 7×7, 7×8, 8×8).

The game is scoped to exactly those 15. Telling a child "there are only 15 left" is motivating, and a small set means each fact gets enough practice to become automatic.

---

## Summary

| # | Finding | Strength of evidence | Where it shows up in the game |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Recalling an answer from memory builds fluency better than re-reading or chanting | Strong; replicated specifically for multiplication | Every question is typed from memory; no multiple choice |
| 2 | Practice spread over days beats massed practice | Strong; hundreds of studies | Short daily missions; facts come back on an expanding schedule |
| 3 | Children move from counting → deriving from known facts → automatic recall, and should be taught the deriving strategies explicitly | Moderate; widely endorsed by math-education researchers | Each chapter opens with a strategy lesson; misses show the strategy, not just the answer |
| 4 | Mixing problem types in practice beats practicing one type at a time | Strong, mostly with older students | Missions mix facts; finished chapters return for review |
| 5 | Timed practice helps fluency, but only once a child is accurate | Supported by the current consensus; historically contested | No visible timer; speed only matters for top ranks, after accuracy |
| 6 | Educational games mostly improve motivation; learning effects are small | Meta-analytic; small effects | The game is the wrapper. The learning comes from #1–#5 |
| 7 | Parents' attitudes and involvement affect math learning | Moderate | Parent setup, a progress table, and a design that rewards playing together |

## 1. Retrieval practice: answer from memory

**What the research says.** Pulling an answer out of memory strengthens that memory more than seeing or repeating the answer does. This "testing effect" is one of the most reliable findings in learning science (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006).

It has been tested with multiplication facts directly. In a 2023 classroom study, second graders in the Netherlands practiced one set of times tables with flashcards (retrieval) and another set by chanting them aloud (restudy). Flashcard practice produced stronger gains in fluency, both right after practice and one week later (Ophuis-Cox, Catrysse & Camp, 2023).

**Caveats.** The flashcard study is small (48 children). Its result fits a large body of retrieval research, though, so the direction of the effect isn't in much doubt.

**In the game.**
- Every question is answered by typing the number on a keypad. There is **no multiple choice**, because picking from options is recognition, not recall.
- After every answer, he sees right away whether it was correct. Retrieval practice works best with feedback.

---

## 2. Spacing: a little, often

**What the research says.** Spreading practice over several sessions produces better long-term memory than the same amount of practice crammed into one session. A large review of the spacing literature covered hundreds of experiments (Cepeda et al., 2006), and spacing has been shown to help children learn multiplication facts specifically (Rea & Modigliani, 1985). Spacing is most powerful when combined with retrieval.

**In the game.**
- A mission is 12 questions, about 6 minutes. The design target is one mission a day.
- Each fact has a rank. Every correct answer pushes its next review further out; a miss brings it back immediately:

| Rank | Next review after a correct answer |
|---|---|
| Recruit (just learned) | Same day |
| Bronze | Next day |
| Silver | 3 days |
| Gold | 1 week |
| Legend | About 3 weeks |

- Missions pick the facts that are most due and least mastered first.
- The debrief screen ends with "Come back tomorrow," not "Play again."

---

## 3. Strategies before speed: from counting to deriving to recall

**What the research says.** Children typically move through three phases with basic facts (Baroody, 2006):

1. **Counting:** skip-counting or finger-counting to find the answer.
2. **Deriving:** using a known fact to work out an unknown one ("5 × 7 is 35, plus one more 7 is 42").
3. **Mastery:** recalling the answer automatically.

Math-education researchers argue that drilling with flashcards tries to jump from phase 1 straight to phase 3, and that teaching the deriving strategies explicitly works better (Kling & Bay-Williams, 2015; Bay-Williams & Kling, 2019). Strategies also give a child a way to rebuild a fact they've forgotten instead of guessing.

The 2025 review in *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* takes the same position from a cognitive-science angle. It calls for explicit instruction in thinking strategies alongside well-structured retrieval practice, rather than treating "memorization" and "understanding" as opposing camps (McNeil, Jordan, Viegut & Ansari, 2025).

**Caveats.** Much of the phase model comes from classroom research and expert consensus rather than large randomized trials.

**In the game.** The target facts are grouped by strategy, not by times table. Each group is a chapter, and its strategy is the team's "superpower":

| Chapter | Team | Strategy | Facts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The Doublers | Double, then double again (and a third time for 8s) | 4×4, 4×6, 4×7, 4×8, 8×6, 8×7, 8×8 |
| 2 | Trio Force | 2 groups + 1 more group | 3×3, 3×4, 3×6, 3×7, 3×8 |
| 3 | Plus-One Squad | 5 groups + the rest | 6×6, 6×7, 7×7 |

- **Every chapter starts with a training lesson.** A dot grid fills in step by step to show the strategy (for 8 × 6: 6 → 12 → 24 → 48). The chapter's missions only open after he solves a practice problem with it.
- **"Review the power"** stays available throughout a chapter.
- **A wrong answer goes to the Training Room**, which redraws that fact using its strategy. He then types the correct answer himself, so the miss ends with a successful recall. The fact also comes back later in the same mission.

## 4. Interleaving: mix it up

**What the research says.** Practice sets that mix problem types lead to better delayed test performance than sets that practice one type at a time, even though mixed practice feels harder in the moment. A study with fourth graders found mixed practice produced much better scores on a test the next day (Taylor & Rohrer, 2010). A preregistered randomized trial across 54 seventh-grade classrooms confirmed the benefit at scale (Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung, 2020). One explanation: mixing forces the learner to decide *which* strategy to use, not just how to execute it.

**Caveats.** Most interleaving research is with students older than eight, and with problem types (like geometry formulas) rather than single facts.

**In the game.**
- Mission questions are shuffled, and the same fact never appears twice in a row.
- The order of factors is randomized (6 × 7 one day, 7 × 6 the next).
- **Review from finished chapters:** while working on Chapter 2, up to 2 of each mission's 10 fact questions come from Chapter 1 heroes that are due for review. So he has to decide whether a problem calls for doubling or "2 groups + 1."

## 5. Timed practice: useful, but only after accuracy

This is the most debated topic in the field, so the game takes a cautious position.

**The concern.** Some educators argue that timed tests cause math anxiety in young children (Boaler, 2014). The strategy researchers in section 3 also warn that frequent timed testing can slow progress toward knowing facts from memory, citing a study of first graders (Henry & Brown, 2008, as summarized by Kling & Bay-Williams). Some intervention providers reject timed activities outright for these reasons (Math Learning Center, 2021).

**The current evidence.** The U.S. What Works Clearinghouse practice guide for elementary math intervention recommends regularly including timed activities as one way to build fluency (Fuchs et al., 2021). A 2024 study of 113 fourth and fifth graders found no significant difference in self-reported anxiety between tasks that were openly timed and tasks that were secretly timed. Harder problems did raise anxiety (Maki et al., 2024; reported by Sawchuk, 2024). It's one study relying on children's self-reports, so it narrows the debate rather than settling it. The 2025 *PSPI* review recommends time-limited practice to nudge children from counting toward fast retrieval, **but only after they are highly accurate** with the facts being practiced (McNeil et al., 2025).

**In the game.** The design follows the "only after accuracy" rule and avoids anything that feels like a test:
- **No timer is ever shown.** Answer time is recorded quietly.
- **Speed only matters at the top.** Recruit → Bronze → Silver depend on correctness alone. Reaching **Gold requires a correct answer in under 3 seconds**, a common working threshold for "recalled, not counted."
- Opening the hero strip mid-question restarts the clock, so interface actions never count against him.
- A planned Speed Trial mode races against the child's own previous best ("ghost"), never other children, and only unlocks for facts at Silver or above.

---

## 6. Games: motivation, not magic

**What the research says.** A meta-analysis of game-based math learning found a small positive effect on achievement compared with traditional instruction (about d = 0.13) (Tokac, Novak & Thompson, 2019). Games show clearer benefits for motivation than for learning itself.

**In the game.** The game layer is there to get a reluctant 8-year-old to do six minutes of good practice every day. The learning mechanisms are #1–#5. Specific choices:
- **A story that ties math to winning.** The villain's shield is made of the 15 facts, and each fact mastered cracks one piece of it. The home screen's "How to win" strip spells out the loop: learn a power → power up its heroes → final battle.
- **One clear next step.** The home screen has a single primary button (Start training, Start mission or Start final battle), so a child never has to figure out what to do.
- **Heroes the child cares about.** Each fact is a hero card whose picture and name are set by the family, ideally drawn by the child.
- **Restraint.** Celebrations happen between questions, not during them. A busy screen competes with the math.

---

## 7. The parent's role

**What the research says.** In a study of 438 first and second graders, children of math-anxious parents learned less math over the school year, but only when those parents helped with homework often (Maloney, Ramirez, Gunderson, Levine & Beilock, 2015). In a randomized study, families who used a math story app together even once a week saw gains in children's math achievement (Berkowitz et al., 2015).

**In the game.**
- **Parent setup:** a progress table showing each fact's rank, typical answer time and next review date, so a parent can see which facts are still being counted.
- The tone everywhere is encouraging: a miss is "needs more training," never a failure.

---

## Limitations

- **This is an n = 1 design.** It was built for one child and tuned by watching him play. It has not been tested as an intervention.
- **Several key studies are small** (the flashcard study had 48 children), even where they agree with larger bodies of research.
- **Thresholds are judgment calls.** The 3-second Gold cutoff, the review intervals and the 80/20 mission mix are reasonable, research-informed choices, not values validated in a study.
